## Discrete energy model and pairwise aggregation

Let x be a token sequence, a rubric or attention vector over its spans, and w the task context. Since text is discrete, the partition function is a sum over sequences (or over a finite candidate space), not an integral: pθ(x|a,w)=exp(−Eθ(x,a,w))/Zθ(a,w), with Zθ(a,w)=Σ_x exp(−Eθ(x,a,w)). Lower energy means a better rubric fit. This is the normalized Boltzmann model used in [[Concept Learning with Energy-Based Models]].

For span i, let uθ(x_i,w) be its unary energy, s_i=σ(a_i) its attention gate, and let gθ(x_i,x_j,w) be a compatibility logit, where larger values mean more compatible spans. Define h_ij=s_i s_j and the pair penalty c_ij=softplus(−gθ(x_i,x_j,w)). The explicit aggregate is Eθ(x,a,w)=Σ_i s_i uθ(x_i,w)+λΣ_{i<j}h_ij c_ij. Thus attended spans contribute unary costs, while a pair contributes in proportion to both gates and incurs a high cost when its compatibility logit is low. This gated pairwise construction makes the relational aggregation explicit and connects energy-based composition to [[A Simple Neural Network Module for Relational Reasoning]]. The energy can be augmented with rubric-specific terms, but its components and weights should be reported so the critic can identify which terms drive a proposed edit.

## Mixture importance sampling and softplus contrastive loss

Use a proposal mixture q(x)=Σ_m ρ_m q_m(x), where ρ_m≥0 and Σ_mρ_m=1. The mixture should cover plausible drafts and have support wherever the target has support. For samples x_r drawn from q, the importance estimate is Ẑθ=(1/M)Σ_r exp(−Eθ(x_r,a,w))/q(x_r). The corresponding normalized-model score is approximated by ℓθ(x)=log[pθ(x|a,w)/q(x)]≈−Eθ(x,a,w)−log Ẑθ(a,w)−log q(x).

To obtain the softplus objective, classify examples as either data or proposal draws with equal class priors. The logit for the data class against the proposal class is ℓθ(x). Binary logistic negative log-likelihood is softplus(−ℓθ(x)) for a data example and softplus(ℓθ(x)) for a proposal example. Hence L^ML=E_{x∼pdata}[softplus(−ℓθ(x))]+E_{x∼q}[softplus(ℓθ(x))]. This is an importance-corrected noise-contrastive objective with an importance-sampled, plug-in estimate of the partition function; it is approximate at finite M. In particular, estimate Z with the full mixture density q(x), not merely the component that happened to generate a sample. Monitor effective sample size and proposal coverage, since a poor mixture makes both Ẑ and the resulting contrastive gradients unreliable. The contrastive perspective is related to [[Training Products of Experts by Minimizing Contrastive Divergence]]; importance-weighted proposals and energy-guided sampling also connect to [[Guided Cost Learning: Deep Inverse Optimal Control]].

## From a critic’s textual gradient to a discrete edit

A token sequence has no ordinary Euclidean gradient. Use a differentiable relaxed representation z=φ(x) for scoring and sampler training, and treat the resulting direction as guidance rather than as an exact gradient on strings. At each iteration: (1) score the current draft and spans under the rubric; (2) return localized instructions, such as qualify span i because it conflicts with span j, or replace a phrase with a supported alternative; (3) have an editor propose a finite set of token or span edits; (4) estimate each proposal’s energy change ΔE and rank or sample edits, for example with probability proportional to exp(−ΔE/T), while retaining a nonzero exploration probability; and (5) apply a selected edit, rescore, and keep an audit trail of the instruction, edit, and energy change.

In the relaxed sampler, the update is z^{k+1}=z^k−(α/2)∇_z E_comp(z^k,a,w)+ω^k, followed by projection or decoding to a valid text x^{k+1}=ProjectText(z^{k+1}). Here ω^k is the exploration perturbation; the projected discrete process is an approximation, not exact Langevin dynamics on token strings. Gradients through stochastic edit rollouts can be estimated with a differentiable relaxation such as a soft token distribution, or with a score-function estimator for sampled edits. This procedure makes the textual-gradient step concrete while extending the localized edit loop of [[ProTeGi: Prompt Optimization with Textual Gradients]] and the feedback-as-differentiation framing of [[TextGrad: Automatic Differentiation via Text]].

## Resolving the short-chain K=10 failure with rollout KL shaping

Lab Notebook #42’s failure mode is that ten unshaped transitions can remain close to their initial drafts, so critiques from those samples underrepresent useful alternatives. Shape the sampler before using its outputs for critique. Let τ=(x⁰,e₀,x¹,…,e_{K−1},xᴷ) be an edit rollout drawn from differentiable sampler Qφ(τ|x⁰), and define a broad reference edit policy Q₀. Choose the stated target path distribution P*(τ|x⁰) proportional to Q₀(τ|x⁰)exp(−βEθ(xᴷ,a,w)); β controls energy guidance. Minimize L^KL=KL(Qφ||P*). Up to a constant independent of φ, this is E_{τ∼Qφ}[βEθ(xᴷ,a,w)+log(Qφ(τ|x⁰)/Q₀(τ|x⁰))]. The energy term steers terminal drafts toward rubric-compatible low-energy regions; the log-ratio term regularizes the edit policy relative to the reference. Differentiate through relaxed edit probabilities, or use a policy-gradient estimator for discrete edits.

Operationally, train or update Qφ against this target, draw K=10 rollouts from the shaped sampler, then send their terminal drafts to the critic and apply the localized edit procedure above. This directly addresses the diagnosed short-chain problem by steering the finite-horizon rollout rather than assuming ten ordinary Langevin steps have mixed. A broad Q₀ and moderate β preserve more variation; an overly concentrated target or an overly strong energy term can collapse diversity. Track distinct edits, coverage, energy, and effective sample size, and adjust β or mix shaped rollouts with reference-policy rollouts if coverage falls. KL shaping improves the specified finite-horizon sampling objective; it does not guarantee exact equilibrium samples or eliminate mode loss. This sampler-and-energy viewpoint is connected to [[Bayesian Learning via Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics]], [[Guided Cost Learning: Deep Inverse Optimal Control]], and [[Lab Notebook #42: Why Short-Run K=10 Langevin Chains Fail Without KL Shaping]].
